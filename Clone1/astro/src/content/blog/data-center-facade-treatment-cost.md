<p>Exterior treatment sits much closer to a landscape budget than an architectural one, which on a $500M build makes it a rounding error. The ranges below show what that means in dollars: what moves the price, what a treatment costs at four facility sizes, and what that is as a share of total construction.</p><h2>What moves the price</h2><p>A treatment is priced from the building rather than from a menu. The measure is treated wall area, and three things move the price per square foot: how intricate the design is, how much preparation the wall needs before anything is applied, and how high crews have to work.</p>

<p>Treatable area is the other variable, and it is not the same as floor area. A 200,000 sq ft single-story building with 40-foot walls has roughly 70,000&ndash;90,000 sq ft of exterior wall, and an ordinance may only require treatment on the faces toward a road or a neighborhood. Both variables come out of the site survey, which is why every project is priced from a survey rather than a rate sheet.</p><h2>What it costs, by facility size</h2><p>These are typical ranges for common designs on concrete or metal-panel walls, using typical treatable wall areas for each class of building.</p><table>

Most purpose-built facilities don&#x27;t look like it.</p><h2>Against the total build cost</h2><p><a href="https://news.constructconnect.com/february-2026-data-center-report-after-record-growth-the-outlook-for-the-year-ahead" target="_blank" rel="noopener">ConstructConnect's February 2026 data center report</a> put the average cost of a U.S. data center in 2025 at $633 million, up 70% over the prior year. Costs have kept climbing since; by <a href="https://news.constructconnect.com/october-2026-data-center-report-spending-reaches-98.5-billion-ytd-construction-costs-rise" target="_blank" rel="noopener">October 2026</a> the average had passed $1 billion, which only makes the shares below smaller.</p>

<p>A full campus comes to roughly 0.3&ndash;2% of that same average. The bespoke outlier, at $25M&ndash;$50M, is 4&ndash;8%: the most complex design, the most difficult substrate and the tallest practical walls, stacked across the largest realistic scope.</p><p>Jackson Metcalf, who leads Gensler's data center practice, framed the proportionality directly in a <a href="https://www.semafor.com/article/01/07/2026/how-tech-companies-are-redesigning-data-centers-to-fight-backlash" target="_blank" rel="noopener">January 2026 Semafor piece</a>: &ldquo;Architecture is like the lowest cost of all the different disciplines of data centers.&rdquo; Exterior treatment is a fraction of architecture. The math follows from there.</p><h2>What the alternatives actually cost</h2><p>The right comparison is against the other options on the evaluation: landscaping and architectural redesign. Both have sticker prices. Neither sticker price fully represents the cost of ownership.</p><p>A perimeter screening buffer&mdash;Leyland cypress or similar conifers&mdash;is the cheapest option to install, but the installed price omits irrigation, replacement cycles, trimming, and inspections over the life of the project. It also omits the biology: a <a href="https://research.fs.usda.gov/treesearch/18718" target="_blank" rel="noopener">Berkeley and Oakland study of newly planted street trees</a> (Nowak, McBride &amp; Beatty, 1990) found 34% dead or removed within two years, an average annual mortality of 19%. Street trees aren&#x27;t screening conifers, but the lesson carries. Replanting cycles are part of the cost model, not an exception to it. Height is a separate problem: Leyland cypress <a href="https://fieldreport.caes.uga.edu/news/leyland-cypress-trees-often-planted-incorrectly/" target="_blank" rel="noopener">plateaus at 40&ndash;60 feet</a> mature, and the tallest data center buildings rise well past that. No screening budget closes that gap.</p><p>Architectural redesign produces better visual outcomes than either alternative, at a different cost and timeline. Design-phase changes to massing or facade character cost the most of the three options, and retrofitting an existing facility costs more still. Either path adds months to a schedule rather than weeks. It makes sense for a specific subset of sites&mdash;downtown locations, strong design review boards, corporate flagship status&mdash;and not for the bulk of the pipeline.</p><p>Exterior treatment sits between landscaping and redesign on cost, and ahead of both on installation timeline. A standard 40,000&ndash;80,000 sq ft project on a concrete substrate typically runs three to six weeks from design sign-off to completion.</p><h2>How the approval conversation tends to go</h2><p>At these numbers, budget authorization tends not to be the actual constraint. A single-building treatment, even at the top of its range, is small enough to sit inside a typical project contingency, and a campus-wide program is still a rounding error against the average build.</p><p>The more revealing number is the cost of the alternative. A three-month permitting delay on a $200M project, at a 6% annual cost of capital, costs $3M in carrying costs alone&mdash;before legal fees, zoning restudies, or community engagement consultants. In 2025, roughly 40% of data center projects that met local opposition <a href="https://heatmap.news/daily/260-billion-data-center-opposition" target="_blank" rel="noopener">didn&#x27;t go forward</a>, by Heatmap&#x27;s count. For a $200M project, that is a $200M outcome risk sitting behind an unresolved aesthetic objection, not a line item. <span class="pc-quote">The expected cost of doing nothing is considerably higher than any treatment scenario above.</span></p>

<p>Most developers who run this comparison reach the same conclusion without much deliberation: against the cost of a stalled approval, treating the wall is the cheap option.</p>
