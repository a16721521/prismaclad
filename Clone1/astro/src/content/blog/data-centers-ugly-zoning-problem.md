<p>Counties across the country now require data centers to be &quot;attractive.&quot; None of them say how.</p><p>That ambiguity matters more than it might seem. Roughly $64 billion in U.S. data center projects were blocked or delayed by community opposition between May 2024 and March 2025, according to <a href="https://www.datacenterwatch.org/report" target="_blank" rel="noopener">Data Center Watch</a>. Jurisdictions from Loudoun County to New Albany are rewriting their codes faster than developers can respond. And the industry&#x27;s default answer to the visual problem&mdash;planting trees and pushing dirt&mdash;is a geometric mismatch with concrete boxes that can outgrow any screening tree, on footprints the size of four football fields.</p><p>The result is a two-tier market. At the cheap end: landscaping that takes decades to mature, dies at measurable rates, and rarely reaches the top of the wall. At the expensive end: full architectural redesign, reserved for the handful of sites where the politics demand it. In between, almost nothing.</p><p>That middle is where the problem actually lives.</p><h2 id="what-codes-say">What the codes say</h2><p>The operative phrase in 2026 is &quot;principal fa&ccedil;ade building treatments.&quot; Loudoun County required them in its <a href="https://www.loudoun.gov/5274/Zoning-Ordinance-Rewrite" target="_blank" rel="noopener">December 2023 zoning rewrite</a>, on all sides adjacent to residential property, without defining what they are. Prince William County requires principal facades that <a href="https://library.municode.com/va/prince_william_county/codes/code_of_ordinances?nodeId=CH32ZO_ARTVOVDI_PT509DACEOPZOOVDI" target="_blank" rel="noopener">&quot;avoid the use of undifferentiated surfaces.&quot;</a> Fairfax County <a href="https://www.mcguirewoods.com/client-resources/alerts/2024/9/fairfax-county-board-adopts-data-center-zoning-changes/" target="_blank" rel="noopener">went further</a>, explicitly stating that required fenestration &quot;can include faux windows&quot;&mdash;a direct green light for surface-applied graphics.</p><p>None of these ordinances require landscaping or architectural redesign specifically. They require an outcome: a building that doesn&#x27;t read as a blank warehouse. The method is left to the applicant.</p><h2 id="why-trees">Why trees don&#x27;t always work</h2><p>Leyland cypress, the default screening conifer in the mid-Atlantic, grows three to four feet per year and <a href="https://fieldreport.caes.uga.edu/news/leyland-cypress-trees-often-planted-incorrectly/" target="_blank" rel="noopener">plateaus at 40 to 60 feet</a> mature. Planted at nursery size, it takes a decade or more to reach screening height, and the largest data center buildings rise past it even then. And that&#x27;s before accounting for biology: Seiridium canker is incurable, bagworms can defoliate mature specimens in a season, and a <a href="https://research.fs.usda.gov/treesearch/18718" target="_blank" rel="noopener">Berkeley and Oakland study</a> of newly planted street trees found 34% dead or removed within two years.</p><p>In practice, landscape mitigation is a rescue operation rather than a long-term plan.</p><figure><img loading="lazy" src="/assets/pc-datacenter-screening-trees.jpeg" alt="Rows of Leyland cypress planted to screen a data center wall that rises well above them." style="width:100%;height:auto;display:block;"/><figcaption>Photo: Greg Kahn</figcaption></figure><h2 id="why-architecture">Why architecture doesn&#x27;t scale</h2><p>Full facade treatment works. Corgan&#x27;s CoreSite CH2 in Chicago, Gensler&#x27;s Microsoft hyperscale facilities in Northern Virginia, Meta&#x27;s Henrico County campus with its tobacco-barn-referencing panels&mdash;these buildings don&#x27;t generate community opposition the way blank concrete boxes do. But they are exceptions triggered by specific conditions: downtown sites, powerful design review boards, corporate flagship status, or a rezoning fight the developer needs to win badly enough to pay for it.</p><p>For most projects, the calculus is different. Permitting in established markets already takes years, and architectural redesign adds to that.</p><blockquote>The big squat buildings are hard to upgrade aesthetically; it&#x27;s always going to be a big flat warehouse-looking building. We also have some clients where their model is not to spend any more money than they have to because it&#x27;s all going into the infrastructure.</blockquote><p>That&#x27;s Mary Hart, mission-critical practice leader at HKS, putting it plainly.</p><h2 id="proof-middle">The proof that something in between works</h2><p>Google commissioned hand-painted murals at data center facilities in Oklahoma, Belgium, Iowa, and Ireland starting in 2016. VP Joe Kava&#x27;s framing <a href="https://blog.google/innovation-and-ai/infrastructure-and-cloud/global-network/introducing-data-center-mural-project/" target="_blank" rel="noopener">at launch</a>:</p><blockquote>Because these buildings typically aren&#x27;t much to look at, people usually don&#x27;t, and rarely learn about the incredible structures and people who make so much of modern life possible.</blockquote><p>The murals didn&#x27;t redesign the buildings. They changed what the buildings communicated.</p><p>PSE&amp;G&#x27;s substation mural program in New Jersey is the more instructive case. The Nye Avenue Substation in Irvington carries nine murals <a href="https://nj.pseg.com/newsroom/newsrelease463" target="_blank" rel="noopener">&quot;commissioned in response to a request from Irvington officials during the substation&#x27;s zoning approval process.&quot;</a> That&#x27;s not a goodwill gesture. That&#x27;s a graphic treatment satisfying a regulatory aesthetic condition, on a utility building, at industrial scale.</p><figure><img loading="lazy" src="/assets/pc-google-mural-datacenter.jpeg" alt="A Google data center exterior bearing a large hand-painted mural." style="width:100%;height:auto;display:block;"/><figcaption>Photo: Google</figcaption></figure><h2 id="solution">What the solution looks like</h2><p>Large-scale surface patterning applied directly to the building exterior.</p><p>No rebuilding, no changes to the building envelope. Painted or vinyl-applied pattern geometry, designed at the scale of the building rather than a signage panel. A structure running 500,000 square feet of exterior wall is a systems problem, and patterning at that scale solves it the same way the building was designed: with precision, repetition, and scale.</p><p>A site that needs to recede gets a treatment derived from what surrounds it. Forest, terrain, sky, stone, sampled and translated into geometric form at facade scale. From the road it reads as landscape, not warehouse. A site in a denser or more visible context gets large-scale patterning that signals the building was designed, not just erected. Both satisfy the specific language planners are writing into codes: surface differentiation, visual articulation, fenestration equivalents.</p><p>The cost sits closer to a landscape budget than an architectural one. On a $500 million build, it is a rounding error.</p><blockquote>We got pushback and the authorities saying, &#x27;You&#x27;re just greying the whole area with blocks. Can you not make them look a little better?&#x27;</blockquote><p>That&#x27;s Lex Coors, Digital Realty&#x27;s chief data center technology officer, describing the demand without meaning to. The answer is on the wall. Literally.</p>
